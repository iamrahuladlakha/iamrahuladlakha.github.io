A judge that agrees with your human reviewers 90% of the time can be completely useless. The first time I worked through why, it changed how I look at every eval dashboard that reports "agreement" as its headline number. This post is my attempt to lay out what an LLM-as-a-judge is, how I'd check that one deserves trust, and what it takes to run one in production without fooling yourself.

By an LLM-as-a-judge I mean a second model that gets three things: the input, your application's output and a written rule. It returns a verdict (pass or fail, a label, or a score) plus its reasoning. That lets you check qualities only language can describe, like "did it answer the question?" or "is every claim supported?", on thousands of outputs a day.

The problem: the checks that matter need reading

Some checks are easy for code. Is this valid JSON? Does it contain a link? Is it under 300 words? Others need someone to read the text. Is the tone right? Does the summary keep the key points? Did the agent invent an appointment time? A person can judge those, but not ten thousand times a day.

Here's the arithmetic that made it concrete for me. A support bot handles 200,000 replies a day. A typical judge call costs somewhere between $0.01 and $0.10. At $0.02 a call, judging 5% of traffic (10,000 replies) costs $200 a day. Ten thousand expert reviews at two minutes each would be 20,000 minutes, about 333 hours of work, every single day. It's the head chef who tastes every plate: perfect quality control at 20 covers a night, impossible at 2,000.

So you write the rule down once, precisely enough that a new colleague would reach your verdicts, and let a model apply it. The catch is that the judge is itself a prompt you wrote. It inherits the blind spots of its model and the vagueness of its rubric, and it can be wrong in quiet ways.

How it works: what goes into a judge

A judge prompt has up to five parts, followed by the evidence for the case being judged. Hover, focus or tap each part below to see what it does and why it's there.

PartWhat it does
ContextWhat the application does, and the domain knowledge this check needs.
One precise criterionA single rule, including what to ignore. "Cites at least one source document. Ignore formatting." beats "Is it high quality?"
Labelled examples (optional)A few pass and fail cases with reasons. Add them only if accuracy needs it, since they cost tokens on every call.
Reasoning first, verdict lastAsks for the reasoning before the verdict. The reasoning is what you read when you disagree.
A way outLets the judge answer "unknown" when information is missing, rather than guess.
EvidenceThe variables filled in per case: the input, the output, the retrieved context or a reference answer.

Beyond the prompt itself, I keep coming back to four design choices:

  • Verdict shape. Prefer binary pass/fail or a category over a 1–5 scale. Nobody can say what separates a 3 from a 4, and models have favourite numbers (GPT-3.5 leans towards 7). A pass/fail can be counted and validated.
  • Scope. One judge per failure mode, not a single "god evaluator". A score of "quality: 6/10" doesn't tell anyone what to fix.
  • Reference. Reference-based judging (compare with an expected answer) where you have labels, as in tests and CI. Reference-free judging (check the output against a rule or against the context it was given) for live traffic, where no expected answer exists.
  • Granularity. For grounding checks, go claim by claim. The judge lists each factual claim and marks it supported or not, and code computes the share. Never ask the judge to do the arithmetic.

That last one deserves an example. Faithfulness (also called groundedness) asks whether every claim in an answer built from retrieved documents is supported by those documents. "Refunds take 5 business days and go to the original payment method" is two claims. If the retrieved policy supports only the first, the score is \(1 \div 2 = 0.5\), and the unsupported claim goes into the score's comment for whoever reads it next.

Measuring the judge: why raw agreement lies

Before trusting a judge you run it on cases a careful person has already labelled and compare. The obvious number is raw agreement: the share of cases where the two match. The trouble shows up whenever one verdict is much more common than the other. Try this one before reading on.

The "always pass" judge on 100 outputsValue
Real failures (labelled by a person)10
Raw agreement with the person90 of 100
Failures it caught0 of 10
Cohen's kappa0

Ninety percent agreement, zero information. The fix is a number that subtracts the agreement you'd get by luck alone. That's Cohen's kappa:

\[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e = a_{\text{fail}} \times j_{\text{fail}} + a_{\text{pass}} \times j_{\text{pass}} \]

In words: \(p_o\) is the share of cases where judge and person agree. \(a_{\text{fail}}\) and \(a_{\text{pass}}\) are the shares the person labelled fail and pass, and \(j_{\text{fail}}\), \(j_{\text{pass}}\) the same for the judge. \(p_e\) is how often they'd agree if the judge picked labels at random in its own proportions. Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for no better than luck, and below 0 for worse than luck. The usual reading is 0.81–1.0 almost perfect, 0.61–0.80 substantial, 0.41–0.60 moderate, and below that fair to slight.

I pair kappa with two numbers that look at the failures directly. Recall is the share of real failures the judge caught. Precision is the share of the judge's "fail" verdicts that were real. Their combination, \(F_1 = 2PR \div (P + R)\), is high only when both are.

The gap between raw agreement and kappa is not a corner case. I recently read Reliability without Validity, a large study of 21 judge models, and on one benchmark raw agreement overstated the judges by 33.8 to 41.3 percentage points compared with kappa. On another it was about 10. How much it flatters you depends on the label mix of your data, which is exactly why I don't want raw agreement as the headline.

Three worked examples

Customer support: was the conversation escalated?

One calibration example I came across used a judge to decide whether a support conversation had been handed to a human. It was run on 35 labelled conversations, 14 escalated and 21 not. The judge flagged 13 of the 14 and none of the 21. Agreement is \((13 + 21) \div 35 = 0.971\). The person says "escalated" for \(14 \div 35 = 0.400\) of cases and the judge for \(13 \div 35 = 0.371\), so \(p_e = 0.400 \times 0.371 + 0.600 \times 0.629 = 0.526\) and \(\kappa = (0.971 - 0.526) \div (1 - 0.526) = 0.94\). Almost perfect. Recall is \(13 \div 14 = 0.929\) and precision \(13 \div 13 = 1.0\).

The part I liked is the one miss. It was a genuinely borderline message ("Forget it, I'll email your CEO directly") where two of the rubric's own rules conflicted. The fix belonged in the rubric, not in a different model.

Education: checking a new marker

The same idea works away from software. A head teacher hands a new teaching assistant the marking scheme for an essay question and marks 40 essays alongside them. The head fails 8; the assistant fails 9, which are 7 of the head's 8 plus 2 the head passed. Agreement is \((7 + 30) \div 40 = 0.925\), which sounds great until you notice that passing everyone would already score \(32 \div 40 = 0.8\). Luck-level agreement is \(0.2 \times 0.225 + 0.8 \times 0.775 = 0.665\), so \(\kappa = (0.925 - 0.665) \div (1 - 0.665) = 0.78\): substantial, not yet almost perfect. Recall \(7 \div 8 = 0.875\), precision \(7 \div 9 = 0.78\), \(F_1 = 0.82\). Reading the 3 disagreements shows the line "uses evidence" was read two ways. One sentence of clarification and a second round of 40, and the assistant is ready. That's the default preset in the calculator above.

Agents: scoring tool-call plans

The third example is the one closest to what I build. AgentJudgeBench scored agents' tool-call plans with six judges against a reference computed in code, and changed one judge setting at a time. What I took from it is that the knobs people argue about most matter least:

Setting changedEffect on alignment with the reference
Thinking mode on vs off (QwQ-32B)at most 0.3 points across all 24 paired cells
Sampling temperature 0.3, 0.7, 1.0at most 0.6 points
Structured per-metric rubric with a JSON verdict, vs one free-form instruction+4.8 to +6.5 points on one pairing; +3.9 / +2.4 / −0.8 (easy / medium / hard) on another
Default verdict of 1.0 vs 0.5 when no fault is found+0.4 to +1.0 for strong agents, +4.1 and +5.6 for the two weakest

So for structural scoring, the prompt's wording and defaults move the result far more than thinking or temperature. The authors are careful that prompt format is "impactful but judge/generator-dependent", so it's something to measure on your own setup rather than a guaranteed win. One more number from the same paper stuck with me: GPT-5.4 judging its own tool-call plans showed no overall self-preference, but over-credited its own call order by 0.172 on a 0–1 scale, against at most 0.008 for other agents' plans.

Calibration as a procedure, not a vibe

Each example above is one round of comparison. In practice I treat calibration like model training, with held-out data, because every look at the judge's mistakes leaks a little of that data into the prompt. One evals guide I came across turns this into fixed proportions. Step through it below.

The short version: label 100–200 examples per failure mode with a trusted domain expert. Keep 10–20% as a pool for few-shot examples, 40–45% as a dev set you iterate against, and 40–45% as a test set you score once, at the end. With 150 labels that's about 22, 64 and 64. Aim for 30–50 passes and 30–50 fails in each of dev and test, and report the true positive rate (failures caught) and the true negative rate (good outputs passed) rather than accuracy, ideally both above 90%. If test comes out much worse than dev, suspect overfitting, though small samples can cause a gap too.

The production perspective

Cost scales with three numbers. Judge spend is volume × sampling rate × cost per call. That's easy to say and easy to forget when someone switches sampling from 5% to 100% "just for this week". I'd put a cost alert next to the quality alert from day one.

Replies per day200,000
Share judged5% = 10,000
Cost per judge call$0.02

Same volume by human review, 2 min eachabout 333 hours

To keep that number down: use code wherever a rule can settle the question, run cheap code checks first and send only flagged cases to the judge, sample live traffic, and judge the one step you care about rather than the whole run. Long contexts cost more on every call, which leads to the next point.

Show the judge less. Give each judge only the parts of the trace its failure mode needs. Extra context can make it worse, not better. The way I'd settle it is an ablation: remove one piece at a time, re-score against the human labels, and leave out anything whose removal doesn't hurt agreement.

Judges score after the fact. Evaluators usually run asynchronously, after the user has already seen the answer. If something has to stop a bad answer in flight, that's a guardrail inside the application, with its own latency budget, not an evaluator. A "safety judge" that runs after the reply has been sent can tell you about a problem, but it can't prevent one.

The same case can get different verdicts. Temperature 0 makes a judge more repeatable, not perfectly so. I version the judge like code (prompt, model and score definition together) and record the version on every score. A changed judge means re-scoring both sides of any comparison.

Whatever you optimise against, you overfit. If prompts are tuned until the judge is happy, the application learns the judge's blind spots. That's Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops measuring well. Fresh human labels every so often are the only defence I know.

Same model or a different one? The usual advice is a different, stronger model, because a judge from the same family tends to miss the same mistakes. The evals guide I mentioned argues that for narrow pass/fail judges the same model is usually fine, since the judge is doing a different task. I land in the middle: measured agreement on held-out labels decides, whichever model it is.

Some well-known biases may be smaller than you think. The Reliability without Validity study measured verbosity bias (rating longer answers higher) as the correlation between the length difference of two answers and the judge's pairwise verdict. All 21 judges scored below 0.011, which means length explains about 0.01% of the verdicts' spread (0.011 squared). The authors don't claim it's gone under every rubric, so I still audit for it, but it's no longer the first thing I'd fix. Position bias (preferring whichever answer is shown first) and marking a claim supported because it's true in general, though the context never said it, still get explicit checks.

The same paper ends with a minimum checklist I'd run before deploying any judge: report a chance-corrected number like kappa; run every pair in both orders; repeat at least 3 runs at temperature 0 with caching off; test on at least two benchmarks, one preference-style and one correctness-style; and if repeat runs agree above 0.95, confirm position bias is below 0.10 before calling the judge reliable, because a judge that gives the same verdict every time can still be giving the wrong one.

What I'd do differently. Label before writing the judge, not after. The tempting shortcut is to tune the prompt against a handful of examples you remember, and that tends to look fine until someone labels a proper test set. I'd also trace every judge call like any other model call, with its input, rendered prompt, reasoning and cost, so a strange verdict can be debugged instead of argued about.

What this means for leaders. A judge model makes quality checks affordable at scale: at $0.02 a call, judging 10,000 replies a day costs about $200, against roughly 333 hours of expert review. The risk is a judge nobody measured, which can report 90% agreement and catch nothing. The decision to make is whether any judge can go live without a chance-corrected agreement score on held-out human labels. Ask your team: what is this judge's kappa, and on how many labelled cases? How many real failures does it catch? Which judge version produced this trend line, and who still reviews a sample by hand?

Takeaways

  • A judge is a second model applying a written rule. Write one precise criterion per failure mode, ask for reasoning before the verdict, and allow "unknown".
  • Prefer pass/fail over 1–5 scales, and claim-by-claim checks with the arithmetic in code.
  • Raw agreement flatters a judge whenever one verdict dominates. Report kappa, plus recall and precision on the failures.
  • Calibrate with train, dev and test splits, and score the test set once.
  • In production, budget volume × sampling × cost per call, version the judge, show it only the context it needs, and keep a human sample running.

I'm curious how other teams handle this. Do you report kappa for your judges, or is raw agreement still the number on the dashboard?
